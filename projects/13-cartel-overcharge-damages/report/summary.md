# How much did the railroad grain cartel overcharge shippers?

**Question.** From 1880 to 1886 the railroads carrying grain from Chicago to the East coast ran a cartel, the Joint Executive Committee. Some weeks it held prices up; in other weeks it broke down into price wars. If shippers could have sued, how much would they have been owed?

**Answer.** About **$7.6 million** in the dollars of the day, on $25.4 million of freight bills in the collusive weeks. Collusion raised the rate about **43%** above the price war level, so roughly **30 cents of every dollar** shippers paid in those weeks was overcharge. A 95% confidence range is $5.9 million to $9.2 million.

**How we know.** We compared the rate in collusive weeks with the rate in price war weeks, after allowing for the normal seasonal pattern and for competition from lake shipping in summer. A second method learned how prices behaved in price wars and predicted what each collusive week would have cost without the cartel. It gives $6.6 million.

**How sure we are.**

* Every reasonable version of the model finds an overcharge between 34% and 55%.
* When we shifted the cartel dates to the wrong weeks (every shift of six months or more), none of 277 fake calendars produced an effect this large. The effect is tied to when the cartel actually operated.
* The code reproduces published results on the same data to three decimal places.

**What could change the number.**

* Prices fell over the period as new railroads joined. Allowing for that drift lowers the estimate to about $6.5 million. This is the biggest open question.
* Price war prices are not the same as normal competitive prices. If wars were unusually deep punishments, the true overcharge is smaller; if wars were mild or brief, so war prices stayed above the normal non collusive level, it is larger.
* The higher price also cut shipments: without the cartel, shippers would likely have sent about 36% more grain. The economic loss from that forgone trade (deadweight loss) is roughly $1.4 million, a separate harm not included above.

**Bottom line.** The cartel overcharge is large, well measured and robust to timing tests. A damages expert would present $7.6 million with a range of $6.4 million to $7.7 million across methods, and would expect the debate to focus on the price trend and on whether price war prices are a fair stand in for competition.

*Historical illustration: the cartel predates US antitrust law, so no such claim was ever possible. Figures are nominal 1880s dollars.*
