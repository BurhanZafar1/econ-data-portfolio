# Deposit betas: one page summary

**Question.** When the Federal Reserve changes interest rates, how much of that change do US banks pass on to depositors, and what does that mean for a bank's funding cost over the next year?

**What we did.** We used public FDIC national average deposit rates (2009 to October 2026) for four products: interest checking, savings, money market and 12-month CDs. For each of the last four Fed cycles we measured the "deposit beta": the deposit rate change as a share of the Fed's change. We also estimated how quickly deposit rates react, separately for rate increases and decreases.

**What we found.**

* **Most of a Fed move never reaches depositors.** During the 2022 to 2023 increases the Fed's rate rose 5.25 points. The average 12-month CD rate rose about one third of that (beta 0.33). Savings rose 0.08 of it, and interest checking about 0.01.
* **Depositors got more in 2022 than in 2015.** CD betas nearly doubled (0.17 to 0.33) and savings betas rose from 0.02 to 0.08. The FDIC changed how it averages rates in 2021, which may explain part of this.
* **Rates rise faster than they fall, but the evidence is weak.** For savings, money market and CDs, the estimated pass-through is larger for increases than for cuts. Since 2021 the difference is not statistically reliable (money market comes closest); over the longer 2009 to 2026 history it is, though that history includes 2020, when deposit rates were already near zero and could not fall.

**What it means for funding cost.** For an illustrative $1 billion of interest bearing deposits (30% checking, 25% savings, 25% money market, 20% CDs), annual interest cost is about $6.3 million today. In 12 months it would be about:

| Fed path over the next year | Annual interest cost |
|---|---|
| Holds at today's rate | $6.5 million |
| Cuts 1 point in four steps | $5.9 million |
| Raises 0.5 point in two steps | $7.1 million |

Cost rises even if the Fed holds, because the September 2026 increase has not fully reached deposit rates yet.

**Caveats.** These are national averages, weighted toward the largest banks. A bank that competes on price, or relies on CDs, will have higher betas; its own call report data should be used before any decision. The deposit mix is an assumption, not a real bank.
